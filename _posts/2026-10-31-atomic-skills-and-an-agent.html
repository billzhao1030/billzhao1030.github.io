Atomic Skills, One Grip, and an Agent: Why We Stopped Training a Cube-Solving Policy

Xunyi Zhao · ~18 min read · simulation results · project page

Every robot that has solved a Rubik's cube so far has done it the same way at heart: an optimal solver writes down twenty-odd moves, and a learned controller is made strong enough to execute whatever that solver says. We went the other way. Two simulated Shadow hands learn twelve face turns, each one forced to finish in the same standard grip; an agent plans the solve the way a person does, with CFOP; an executor chains the turns. Nine random scrambles in a row, nine solved. This post is about why that decomposition is the right one, what it took to make it hold, and where it still does not.

01Two robots, one assumption

OpenAI's Dactyl (2019) is the landmark: one Shadow hand, a recurrent policy trained in simulation with automatic domain randomization at a scale few labs can afford, executing the face rotations and flips that Kociemba's solver produces, on a real robot [1]. A paper from this month puts an ANYmal-D quadruped on a 0.4 m, 4.6 kg cube: an RGB-D pipeline reconstructs the cube, Kociemba's two-phase solver plans, and four learned whole-body skills (two flips, two two-layer rotations) plus locomotion carry it out; it reports three hardware solves of roughly thirty steps in eight to nine minutes [2]. Both are real-world systems, which ours is not yet. That deserves saying first.

But look at what both take for granted. The plan is an optimal solver's output, and the controller is shaped to that plan: Dactyl's policy must survive any sequence of face turns and flips the solver throws at it, so the burden of generality falls on one network and its training budget; the quadruped's skill set is whatever the giant cube and Kociemba's move alphabet require. Neither design has a place where a better plan, a different method, or a bigger cube can come in without retraining the part that touches the cube. That is the assumption we wanted to drop.

02The bet: a contract, not a policy

Our hands hold the cube the way a cuber does — left thumb and middle finger pinching the F and B centres, right thumb on the FR edge, right middle and ring fingers behind, the index fingers resting behind the top corners. We call this the standard grip, and everything rests on one rule about it:

The standard grip, held still while the camera circles it. Both UR10e arms are mirror-symmetric; only the hands touch the cube.

Every atomic move starts in the standard grip and must end in it. A move is not "turn R"; it is "turn R and come home". Only then does it count.

That rule is what turns a pile of skills into a vocabulary. If every move leaves the hands in the same place, any sequence of moves is executable by concatenation; the planner never has to know what the fingers are doing, and the skills never have to know what comes next. The cost is paid once, per move, at training time.

Concatenation in practice: R U R' U' scrambles the cube, its inverse brings it back — eight moves back to back, each one ending in the grip. Left: first person; right: wide view.

(Swipe the figure sideways.) The agent never sees a finger; the skills never see the plan. The standard grip is the interface between them.

03The twelve moves

Each face turn is a hand-designed reference trajectory — which fingers move, where the wrist goes, the order of releases — plus a policy trained with PPO to output small corrections on top of it. The reference gets the structure right; RL makes it robust to the contact physics we cannot script. The arms' shoulders and elbows never move. Success is measured over 512 games in a fixed environment: the layer must stop within 90°±3° and the hands must be back in the standard grip (fingertips within 8 mm, cube within 8 mm and 8° of where it started).

04What it actually took to make moves come home

Getting a layer to 90° was the easy part; most moves did that in a day. Getting the hands back took weeks, and the lessons are the most transferable thing in this project.

A way back only works from the starts it was trained on. When a move ends with the cube within about 5 mm and 5° of home, training the way back directly on that move's real end states — not on a clean idealised start — reaches 100% in a few dozen iterations; that is how B's way back went from 81.6% to 100%. When the move leaves the cube 10 mm or 15° off, no amount of RL on the way back recovers it. We tried long two-stage returns, "wrench" regrips, re-centring through other grips; none of them moved the number.

So fix the move, not the way back. Two examples. The index finger flicking B used to drag the whole cube 9 mm sideways; having the right thumb pull the cube the other way during the flick kept it within 4.6 mm, and B's chain went from 6% to 100%. F was worse: the index pressed on the middle of the UFR corner's top sticker, and most of that force pointed at the cube's centre, shoving the whole cube. Moving the contact out to the corner made the push mostly tangential; the cube's offset after F dropped from 9.1 mm to 4.5 mm and F went from 0% to 98.6% end to end. Nothing about the learning changed. The geometry of the contact did.

Mirrors are free. L, L', B' and F' are not trained at all: they are the mirror images of R', R, B and F, run on the mirrored grip with the policy's observations and actions reflected, followed by a short regrip back to the standard grip. Four of twelve moves cost nothing beyond the regrip.

L U L' R on the executor: for L and L' the hands pass through the two-handed grip into the mirrored grip, run the mirrored policy, and come back. Stickers are right after every move.

Check rarely. A person solving a cube looks at it a handful of times, not after every turn. Our executor checks the stickers and the grip only at the end of each CFOP stage — seven times a solve — and between moves only asks whether the cube is still in hand and the layer is not stuck halfway. Measuring the grip after every move had cost 32 s per move; it was also unnecessary.

05The agent: CFOP, rewritten for hands

The planner solves the way speedcubers do: white cross, four first-two-layer pairs, orient the last layer, permute it. The published CFOP formulas are written for human wrists and use whole-cube rotations (x, y, z), wide turns and slice turns. We rewrote every one of them into the twelve face turns: a wide or slice turn is one or two face turns plus a rotation, and a rotation can be pushed to the end of the formula by relabelling the faces after it and then dropped. Every rewrite is re-checked on a simulator. All 119 cases — 41 F2L, 57 OLL, 21 PLL — have a version the hands can do.

The planner then chooses among equivalent formulas by what the hands are good at, not by length: each move's cost is −log of its measured success plus 0.1 × its seconds. F' and B' take 11.8 s each, so the planner only spends them when they save several fast turns. On four random scrambles, motion time per solve fell from an average of 393 s to 240 s, with every checkpoint still passing.

06A language model can be the agent

Because the interface is a list of face turns, the planner is replaceable. We gave the job to an Opus session through claude -p: for each CFOP stage it sees the cube, is told the stage's goal and the face turns the hands can do, and may try a sequence on a copy of the cube before answering. It gets no formula library and no hints from our planner. Its answer is checked on the logical cube and handed to the executor.

The hands then executed the model's own 93-move solution: every checkpoint passed. The weak spot is honest and visible in the replay on the project page: the model does not know the F2L cases by heart, so it works each pair out by trial — 42 to 56 tool turns per solve. A person who has looked at the cube from all sides and knows the cases does it in about seven decisions. Closing that gap (letting the model look around first, check once, and recall cases instead of searching) is our next agent experiment.

07One network for all of it

Twelve moves, each with several segments, trained as separate policies is still a lot of networks. On a second machine we distil all of them into one policy that receives the move and segment as input: 58 segments, a 418-dimensional observation, 44 actions. It first imitates each move's own policy, then trains with PPO on all of them at once. Measured the same way — whole move, 512 games, home or not — the shared network currently matches the dedicated policies at 100% on 9 of 13 moves (R, R', U, L, L', D', F', B', x'). The remaining four fail at a way back or at a flick whose start the shared network leaves slightly different from what the reference expects. A finding worth keeping: per-segment scores did not predict whole-move success at all — 0.755 vs 0.760 per segment hid a drop from six moves passing to three — so we only select checkpoints by whole-move results.

08Why this is the path to 4×4 through 7×7

Here the argument stops being about one cube. Big cubes are solved by reduction (the Yau method): build the centres, pair the edges, then finish as a 3×3. Our sticker-only Yau planner solves random 4×4 to 7×7 scrambles — 20 of 20 in our benchmark, five per order — and it is worth looking at what it asks for:

Three quarters of every big-cube solve is the moves the hands already have. What is new is one family — turn a single inner layer — and a few rotations. Under our design that is a handful of additional atomic skills, each trained once against the same standard-grip contract, and the planner does the rest. Under an end-to-end design it is a new cube, a new action distribution, and a new training run at full cost. That asymmetry is the whole case for atomic skills plus an agent: the expensive part (contact-rich control) is small, local and reusable; the part that grows with the problem (search over a huge state space) is exactly what planners and language models are good at.

To be clear about the status: the 4×4–7×7 numbers above are from the planner alone. The hands have not executed a big-cube solve, and the inner-layer skill has not been trained.

09What is not done

It is simulation. Physics is MuJoCo Warp, rendering is Isaac Sim, the cube is a carefully modelled 56 mm puzzle with realistic turning torque and friction — but no real hand has touched it. The robots in [1] and [2] are real; ours is not yet.

Segments are snapped. The executor starts each move segment from that segment's clean, trained start, so in the recordings the hands jump a few millimetres between segments (up to 8 mm in the worst place, inside F'). It is a crutch for the remaining drift, and we show it rather than hide it.

Rotations are mostly unsolved. x' works (93.8%): the right wrist turns R' with the cube locked, the left ring finger slides underneath, and the hands land back in the grip. y' turns all the way with a relay between the hands and its way home is improving, but it is not yet reliable; y, z, z' and x follow from it. The 3×3 solver does not need them — it was rewritten without rotations — but human-style big-cube solving will.

x' (93.8%): the right wrist turns R' with the whole cube locked, the left ring finger supports it from below, and both hands land back in the standard grip — green front becomes yellow front. Left: first person; right: wide view.

Perception is assumed. The agent is handed the cube state, or ideal renders of it. Real cube-state estimation, as both prior systems built, is still to do.

10The takeaway

The interesting design decision in a dexterous system is not the size of the policy; it is where you draw the interface. Draw it at "any sequence an optimal solver emits" and every bit of generality has to be bought with training. Draw it at "a small set of moves that always come home" and generality is free above the line: a new method, a new planner, a language model, a bigger cube. Below the line, the work is real and it is specific — where a thumb pushes, which way a fingertip slides — but it is finished once per move.
